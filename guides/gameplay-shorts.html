How to cut gameplay without losing the story.

A practical guide to finding your own plays, building context around big moments and avoiding misleading game highlights.

7 min reading time

The most common mistake: picking action instead of achievement

Imagine a 20-second team fight in a multiplayer game. Explosions fill the screen, three opponents fall, and a large victory banner appears. That may look like the perfect highlight. But if the recording belongs to a player who was eliminated five seconds before the fight, the footage may be documenting the team rather than that person's own play.

That distinction matters. A personal gaming montage promises viewers a story about what the player did. A team recap can legitimately feature teammates. Neither is automatically better; the editor must first choose the purpose.

Step 1: decide whose story you're telling

Before placing an IN point, identify the target: your own play, a whole team's win, or a funny moment. For a personal montage, look for information tied to the player: character position, ability use, health bar, camera behavior and the moment of elimination. If a death screen begins, ask whether the useful action happened before that point.

Concrete example: A player enters a fight at 02:13, dodges a skill at 02:16, secures a double kill at 02:20, dies at 02:22, and watches their teammates finish the fight at 02:32. A personal highlight might reasonably run from 02:12 to 02:22. A team-fight recap could keep the later action, but it should not be described as the player's own triple kill.

Step 2: include enough lead-in to understand the payoff

Clipping exactly when a damage number appears often makes an impressive play look accidental. A viewer needs a beat of setup: the dangerous position, the bait, a dodged projectile or the decision to engage. For many moments, one to three seconds of lead-in is enough; sometimes a longer setup is the entire point.

  1. Scrub backwards from the payoff until you can see the decision that made it possible.
  2. Set the IN point just before that decision, removing idle movement that doesn't change the meaning.
  3. Play the segment to a person unfamiliar with the match. Can they explain what happened?

Step 3: end after the reaction, not during it

One common automated-editing mistake is holding the clip for eight seconds after the actual highlight. Unless a reaction, score confirmation or second payoff adds value, those seconds dilute the moment. Keep a brief aftermath so the viewer registers success, then cut.

A strong short isn't merely the highest-action interval. It's the shortest segment that lets the viewer understand why the moment mattered.

Step 4: sequence multiple highlights

When you have three clips, don't automatically arrange them chronologically. Consider an opening that raises a question, a middle clip that establishes skill and a final clip with the most satisfying payoff. ClipForge Studio lets you add multiple intervals from one source and move them up or down.

Step 5: protect the game information

With 16:9 gameplay, a full 9:16 crop can remove the player, minimap or kill feed. Before exporting, switch between Fill and Blur + fit. If the game uses a wide HUD, preserving the full frame may be more useful than maximizing every pixel of the video. Learn more in our framing guide.

A 30-second quality checklist

Watch the video once with sound and once without it. Ask: Is the featured player actually doing something? Can a stranger identify the payoff? Does the first second give a reason to watch? Is the final second necessary? If removing a section doesn't change the story, remove it.
